In this essay, I will present briefly and discuss the paper "BERT: Pre-training of Deep Bidirectional Transformers for Language Understanding", by J. Devlin et al. (Google AI Language), introduced in 2018.

I chose this article as I was introduced to it during the ALTEGRAD course. I thought the architecture and the ideas were interesting, going aside from the generative models. The article compares its own model with GPT-1. I also thought that it would be the perfect occasion to learn from the very first GPT model from OpenAI to understand better how LLMs scaled so quickly and so far. 

Firstly, BERT stands for "Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers", and aims at introducing a broad understanding of a sentence as a whole rather than focusing on the previous word in the sentence.
The paper focuses on making a comparison with GPT-1 from OpenAI and ELMo. GPT-1 is a model trained with a loss that requires the causal architecture. In other words, OpenAI relies on a loss such that the current token that is being processed by the model can only see its predecessors. I think the comparison with BERT is quite natural as the two models are meant for the same NLP tasks. The article shows, and it is expected, that a bidirectional context will help the model to perform much better in tasks such as text understanding or classification compared to GPT-1 and its left-to-right self-attention. 
The true feat of the article is to come up with a loss that allows an unsupervised training that allows a non-triangular attention matrix. Devlin et al. decided to go with a 15% random masking of the tokens of the text so that the model is trained to retrieve those tokens, knowing the tokens at its right AND at its left. I think it is a quite ingenious proposition of a loss function. The choice of a 15% masking rate is surprisingly not thoroughly studied in the paper. It seems to me that it may be one of the main hyperparameters that drives the capability of the model to understand the language since it directly controls the share of tokens the model is training on, whereas, for GPT-1, every token is a direct target.  

As my training also includes civil and environment engineering too, it is quite surprising to read articles lacking proper figures. It is even more surprising as a mathematics student to read such an important paper with such a low amount of mathematical formula. Somehow, the article feels much more like an engineering report rather than a mathematical development. 
I note that the paper has 5 figures to illustrate the architecture but none of them is very clear to me. It certainly lacks some sort of global diagram of the architecture and the training process. I think the article would gain substantially in clarity if they provided a quick reminder of the Transformers (still new in 2018 and not as popular as it is nowadays) as well as giving more specific figures about the architecture, especially the choice to stick only with the encoder part, even though it is clearly indicated to refer to the work of Vaswani et al. on Transformers in Attention is all you need.  

In addition, at first, I found it really interesting and surprising that Google AI Language chose to partly train their BERT models on Wikipedia. But in the end, it appears to be a logical choice since Wikipedia is an openly licensed (that means the website can be used for commercial purposes!), high quality text (human corrected), rather unbiased (or at least trying to stick to the fact) and easy access. What really is the main issue in terms of data usage and ethics is the second corpus. 
The second dataset that has been used is BookCorpus, a dataset that has been largely criticized for scraping data without consent and deliberately ignoring the fact that the books were not openly licensed. A striking point is that it can clearly be interpreted as a sort of pillage of common knowledge, or at least as one of the first step toward it.
The paradigm hasn't changed: as LLMs are getting more and more hungry for resources, the company lawsuit "Bartz vs. Anthropic" showed that Anthropic started to massively buy books to scan them as well as pirated some to provide them as high quality data to Claude models. It questions us on our behavior toward data and how we, as a society, protect them. I recognize that LLM requires an unimaginable amount of data to become more and more relevant for a large scale of tasks, but does it justify to destroy books, exploit non open-source data and violate right of authors for commercial purpose? 
One thing is for sure: even if the big tech main figures are publicly calling for a general slowdown on the development of AI, the almost-monthly release of ever more powerful and capable models tells another story. 

What is also really interesting in this paper is that further evidence of some sort of a scaling property has been highlighted as the paper compares different sizes of BERT models and shows that the larger the better. More specifically the article specifies that a larger model can massively improve the results on small scale tasks, such as classification for instance. In a sense, it is further evidence of a general language processing that can be handled through a single architecture, simply by finetuning the model completed with a small task-specific head.     

As a first experiment, I would go for a different set of hyper-parameters such as the pre-training hyper-parameters or dropout values. Since BERT introduces hyperparameters, which, for some of them, are very similar to GPT-1, it might be a good idea to pursue toward a fair comparison with the latter.
Another hyperparameter that seems worth modifying is the masking rate. Why not try 30%, 50% and even 80% and assess the performance of the model with the same conditions? An original approach might be to induce a different masking rate relative to the processed token, as we could go for a higher masking rate on the right and a lower one on the left to try to partly replicate the causal framework?  
More broadly, some experiments could simply consist in trying to modify some specific parts of the architecture: replace the GeLU with another activation function, add or reduce the number and the size of attention layers.
Another interesting experiment would be to try to assess the BERT model with the modern benchmark (that I assume are harder than the 2018 ones). It could allow one to understand the great progress of LLMs in 8 years. One way to observe it would be to benchmark this model with its successors which are RoBERTa (improvement in the training methods and more data) and ModernBERT (modern architecture, more data). This would allow one to comprehend the data impact when comparing BERT and RoBERTa and the architecture impact when comparing RoBERTa/BERT with ModernBERT. One could run those benchmarks on GLUE, MTEB or some coding and reasoning tasks (BERT is not trained for coding or reasoning).  
I would also try to train the model on the BookCorpus dataset only, since GPT-1 is trained only on this one. It would allow one to compare more fairly to GPT-1 as well as get a deeper understanding of what is the value of the data quantity for pre-training. 


To conclude, BERT remains a valuable model and subject of work as it reintroduces the full bidirectional attention to satisfy a bidirectional, masking loss to allow unsupervised training. It brings insights on the historical development of LLM and gives some useful properties such as the fact that a larger backbone can lead to better results even for small representations task that will influence largely the next generation of models.
